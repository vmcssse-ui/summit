Self, Death & the Afterlife

One unit, one argument — told three times. What can death actually take? What is life for, what could survive dying, and what awaits whatever survives?

The
big
question

Does human life have purpose because there is continuing personal existence after death — and what exactly would continue?

01

The afterlife spectrum

Every debate in this unit lines up on an axis — from the literal and external at one end (bodily resurrection; real places of judgement) to the re-described and internal at the other (the psyche; states of mind; God's memory). Pick a sub-topic, then tap a position to see what it claims and where it's strong or weak.

The thread that ties it together

Top answers don't treat the three debates separately. The same questions run through all of them — spot the thread and you're writing at the top of the mark scheme.

Does meaning need an afterlife?

Purpose asks it directly; resurrection and judgement decide whether the answer can be yes. The Nov 2021 question lives here.

What carries 'you'?

Body, body-and-soul reunited, a transformed spiritual body, the soul alone — or nothing. Every position is an answer to this.

Literal or re-described?

Bodily → spiritual → psychological → God's memory. At each step, ask what survives the translation — and when it stops being the doctrine.

04

How to revise this unit

Use your resources in this order. The Philosopher Companion is where you learn to plan both question types.

Watch the lesson & listen to the podcast

One podcast per sub-topic — a teacher and student talk each view through to a judgement. Annotate as you listen.

Complete the greyscale booklet

Read the given text, write the key claims in your own words, then decide the strength and weakness you'd give each view.

Match & apply the key terms

In the Companion: match each term to its meaning, then use it in a sentence. RAG-rate the mastery checklist with the three faces.

Plan AO1, then build AO2 on top

Use the Companion frameworks and the guidance-left / your-turn-right scaffolds. Never evaluate what you haven't first explained.

AO1 · Examine the views (10)
What it is — the factual chain: define the terms, then set out each view in order and apply the evidence (e.g. Genesis 2:7 or 1 Corinthians 15).
Aim — explain the whole range accurately and neutrally. No judging yet.
AO2 · Evaluate the claim (15)
What it is — a three-paragraph dialectic: view, then evaluation → counter-evaluation → implication; mirror it; then judge.
Aim — weigh the views, use one as a lens on another, and reach a justified judgement that links back to your thesis.
