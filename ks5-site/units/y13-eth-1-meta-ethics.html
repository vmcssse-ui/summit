Meta-ethics: the meaning of right & wrong

One unit, one argument — told four times. Meta-ethics does not tell you what to do. It asks a harder, prior question: what are you actually saying when you say something is good?

The
big
question

What do we MEAN when we call something good or right — and can a moral claim ever be a fact?

01

The cognitivism spectrum

Every theory in this unit lines up on one axis: how much factual content does a moral claim have? At the left, moral claims describe the world and can be true or false. At the right, they describe nothing at all. Pick a sub-topic, then tap a position to see what it claims — and the trade it is making.

MOST factual — moral claims describe the world LEAST factual — moral claims describe nothing

Tap a circle — or use Tab and Enter — to explore each position.

The thread that ties it together

This is not four separate topics. It is ONE argument, asked four times: are moral values FACTS? Spot the thread and you are writing at the top of the mark scheme.

One question, four times

The Map sets up the question, DCT answers it with a supernatural fact, Naturalism with a natural fact, and Non-naturalism with a non-natural fact — until the non-cognitivists say there is no fact at all.

The is–ought gap runs underneath

Hume's gap sits under all four sub-topics. Every theory here either tries to cross it (naturalism, DCT), declares it uncrossable (Moore), or gives up on facts entirely (Ayer, Hare).

The standing trade

The more measurable a moral 'fact' is, the less it looks like goodness; the more it looks like goodness, the less measurable it is. Every position on the spectrum is a different price paid for that trade.

02

The same question, four times

Four sub-topics, four big questions — but each one is really asking whether a moral claim states a fact, and if so, what KIND of fact. Each panel gives you the two opposed answers you must be able to set against each other.

Sub-topic 1 · The Meta-ethical Map

Cognitivism vs non-cognitivism

What KIND of question is “What does good mean?” — and why does the answer change everything?
Answer A · Cognitivism

Moral claims ARE about facts, so they can be true or false and can be known. Naturalism, non-naturalism and DCT all sit here.

VS
Answer B · Non-cognitivism

Moral claims state no facts at all — they express feeling, wish or prescription, so they are neither true nor false. Ayer and Hare sit here.

DescriptiveHow people actually behave — pure fact, no 'ought'.
NormativeFirst-order: how we ought to behave.
AppliedNormative principles cashed out in a real case.
Meta-ethicsSecond-order: what the moral words MEAN.
Sub-topic 2 · Divine Command Theory

The Euthyphro Dilemma

If God commands it, does that make it right — or does God command it because it already was?
Horn 1 · Voluntarism

It is right BECAUSE God commands it. God's will constitutes morality (Ockham) — but then 'God is good' says only 'God does what God does'.

VS
Horn 2 · Independent standard

God commands it BECAUSE it is right. Goodness stands independently — but then God is not the source of morality, and omnipotence is lost.

Classical DCT'Good' MEANS commanded by God — real, non-natural, revealed.
OckhamBite the bullet: whatever God willed would be good.
EssentialismGod does not invent or obey goodness — God IS it.
Secular replyObedience for a reward is not autonomous moral choice.
Sub-topic 3 · Ethical Naturalism

Can 'good' be a natural fact?

Can 'good' be reduced to a fact about the world — and is pleasure that fact?
Answer A · Yes, it reduces

'Good' MEANS a natural property — the balance of pleasure over pain (Bentham), higher-quality happiness (Mill), agape, or human flourishing (Foot, Midgley, Warnock). Observable, measurable, objective.

VS
Answer B · No, that's the fallacy

Identifying 'good' with any natural property is the naturalistic fallacy — an 'ought' smuggled out of an 'is' (Hume, Moore). No natural fact can BE goodness.

BenthamQuantity of pleasure — the hedonic calculus.
MillHigher and lower pleasures, plus useful rules.
The familyUtilitarianism · Situation Ethics · Virtue Ethics · Natural Moral Law.
Neo-naturalismHuman flourishing fills the is–ought gap with further facts.
Sub-topic 4 · Non-naturalism & the non-cognitive turn

A fact you cannot define — or no fact at all?

If 'good' cannot be defined, can a moral claim be true at all — or is it just a shout?
Answer A · Real but non-natural

'Good' is simple and unanalysable, like yellow (Moore). It names a real, non-natural property known by intuition — still cognitivist, still true or false.

VS
Answer B · Not a statement at all

'Murder is wrong' is an emotional ejaculation (Ayer) or a universal prescription (Hare) — it names no property, so it is neither true nor false.

MooreThe Open Question argument and the naturalistic fallacy.
IntuitionismWe just SENSE that torturing an innocent is wrong.
RossPrima facie duties; intuition settles the conflicts.
Ayer & HareEmotivism and Prescriptivism — the non-cognitive turn.
Mark-losers

Four mistakes that cost the most marks

Examiners see these four every series. Fix them now — each one is worth more than any extra quotation you could learn.

Intuitionism is non-cognitive.

WrongIt is COGNITIVE and NON-NATURAL. Moore and Ross hold that moral claims are true or false and state real facts — we just know them by intuition rather than observation.

Divine Command Theory is naturalist because God made nature.

WrongDCT is NON-naturalist. The good is grounded in a supernatural being — literally 'above the natural' — not in facts about nature or human nature.

Meta-ethics tells you what to do.

WrongIt is second-order. It asks what the moral words MEAN and how moral claims could be justified. 'What should I do?' is a first-order, normative question.

The naturalistic fallacy proves naturalism false.

ContestedAttfield argues Moore assumed his own conclusion — that no successful definition would ever be produced. Neo-naturalists (Foot, Midgley, Warnock) claim to fill the is–ought gap with further facts about flourishing.

04

How to revise this unit

Use your resources in this order. The Philosopher Companion is where you learn to plan both question types — and meta-ethics is a unit where precision of language is worth more than volume.

Watch the lesson & listen to the podcast

One podcast per sub-topic — a teacher and student talk each position through to a judgement. Annotate as you listen.

Complete the greyscale booklet

Read the given text, write the key claims in your own words, then decide the strength and weakness you would give each position.

Master the 24 key terms — then place every theory on the map

Cognitivist or non-cognitivist? Natural or non-natural? If you cannot place a theory on the spectrum above, you do not yet know it. Use the flashcards in section 06.

Plan AO1, then build AO2 on top

Use the Companion frameworks and the guidance-left / your-turn-right scaffolds. Never evaluate what you have not first explained.

Write to time, then use the review decks

Sit one AO1 and one AO2 under exam conditions, mark against the AQA levels, then close the gaps with the review lessons.

AO1 · “Examine…” (10 marks)
What it is — the factual chain. Define the terms, then set out each position in order and show how one leads to the next: cognitivism → naturalism → the naturalistic fallacy → non-naturalism → non-cognitivism.
Aim — explain the whole range accurately and neutrally, with named scholars and precise vocabulary. No judging yet.
AO2 · “Evaluate this claim.” (15 marks)
What it is — a three-paragraph dialectic: state the view, then evaluation → counter-evaluation → implication; mirror it for the opposing view; then judge.
Aim — weigh the positions, use one as a lens on another (Moore refutes the naturalist; Ayer then turns on Moore), and reach a justified judgement that links back to your thesis.
Paper 1 · the 25-mark dialogue questions
What it is — the connections question: AO1 (10) plus AO2 (15) on how philosophy of religion and ethics speak to each other — e.g. whether the Euthyphro Dilemma damages religious ethics as a whole.
Aim — argue across the two halves of the paper rather than repeating one topic. Meta-ethics is the natural bridge: it is the level at which religious and secular ethics actually meet.
