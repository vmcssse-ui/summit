Conscience

One unit, one argument — told three times. Where does moral authority really come from: reason, society, the psyche — or God?

The
big
question

Should conscience always be obeyed — even when, in the end, it is wrong?

01

The authority spectrum

Every debate in this unit lines up on the same axis — from a source inside the individual (reason, intuition, God speaking within) to a source outside it (upbringing, society, conditioning). Pick a sub-topic, then tap a position to see what it claims and where it's strong or weak.

The thread that ties it together

Top answers don't treat the three debates separately. Three ideas run through all of them — spot the thread and you're writing at the top of the mark scheme.

Inside or outside?

Every view sits somewhere between a source wholly INSIDE the individual (reason, intuition) and wholly OUTSIDE it (society, upbringing). The further outside, the weaker its claim to correct us.

The reformer problem

Durkheim and Freud struggle to explain the prophet, the abolitionist, the whistle-blower — a conscience that condemns the very group or upbringing that shaped it.

The need for an external check

Butler and Aquinas both say: follow conscience always, even in error. Applied to real cases, the unit asks whether something OUTSIDE conscience is needed to catch its worst mistakes.

04

How to revise this unit

Use your resources in this order. The Philosopher Companion is where you learn to plan both question types.

Watch the lesson & listen to the podcast

One podcast per sub-topic — a teacher and student talk each view through to a judgement. Annotate as you listen.

Complete the greyscale booklet

Read the given text, write the key claims in your own words, then decide the strength and weakness you'd give each view.

Match & apply the key terms

In the Companion: match each term to its meaning, then use it in a sentence. RAG-rate the mastery checklist with the three faces.

Plan AO1, then build AO2 on top

Use the Companion frameworks and the real AQA questions in the Exam Questions organiser. Never evaluate what you haven't first explained.

AO1 · Examine the views (10)
What it is — the factual chain: name the accounts the question implies, then set out each in order — Kohlberg's stages, Freud's super-ego, Aquinas's synderesis, whichever the question names.
Aim — explain the whole range accurately and neutrally. No named scholars in the specification — teach the "representative cross-section," but don't name them in an exam answer's thesis as if the spec required it.
AO2 · Evaluate this claim (15)
What it is — a three-paragraph dialectic: view, then evaluation → counter-evaluation → implication; mirror it with a second view; then judge.
Aim — weigh the accounts, use one as a lens on another (does Aquinas survive Freud's challenge?), and reach a justified judgement that links back to your thesis.
