Evil & Suffering

One unit, one argument — asked four times. The problem stated at full strength, then three responses: the Free Will Defence, Hick's soul-making, and Griffin's Process Theodicy.

The
big
question

Can an all-powerful, all-loving God have a sufficient reason for allowing the evil we actually see?

The thread that ties it together

Top answers don't treat the four lessons separately. Three ideas run through all of them — spot the thread and you're writing at the top of the mark scheme.

The evidential problem outlives every answer

The triad can be answered; Ivan's returned ticket and Rowe's fawn cannot. Every response solves the logic better than the evidence.

Greenberg's test

No statement should be made that would not be credible in the presence of burning children. Hold every theodicy to it.

Every response sacrifices something

Plantinga gives up truth-claims, Hick gives up testability, Griffin gives up omnipotence. Name the sacrifice and the essay opens up.

The unit splits into four lessons. They look different, but each is really asking whether God has a sufficient reason for evil.

04

How to revise this unit

Use your resources in this order. The Philosopher Companion is where you learn to plan both question types.

Watch the lesson & listen to the podcast

One podcast per sub-topic — a teacher and student talk each position through to a judgement. Annotate as you listen.

Complete the greyscale booklet

Read the given text, write the key claims in your own words, then decide the strength and weakness you'd give each position.

Match & apply the key terms

In the Companion: match each term to its meaning, then use it in a sentence. RAG-rate the mastery checklist with the three faces.

Plan AO1, then build AO2 on top

Use the Companion frameworks and the guidance-left / your-turn-right scaffolds. Never evaluate what you haven't first explained.

AO1 · Examine the views (10)
What it is — the factual chain: define the problem, then set out each position in order and apply the evidence (e.g. Rowe's fawn, the Holocaust).
Aim — explain the whole range accurately and neutrally. No judging yet.
AO2 · Evaluate the claim (15)
What it is — a three-paragraph dialectic: view, then evaluation → counter-evaluation → implication; mirror it; then judge.
Aim — weigh the positions, use one as a lens on another, and reach a justified judgement that links back to your thesis.
